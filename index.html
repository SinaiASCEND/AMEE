An Integrated Core-and-Cluster Framework for Teaching Physical Examination Aligned with Clinical Reasoning

Students learn how to do the maneuvers. They are far less reliable at deciding which ones a particular patient needs.

R. P. Soriano · J. Hojsak · M. Herscher · B. Storch · B. Forsyth · C. Strother
Leni and Peter W. May Department of Medical Education · Icahn School of Medicine at Mount Sinai, New York

What we did

One school's implementation of Core + Cluster, and some early data

We adapted the Core + Cluster model described by Gowda and colleagues for an early clinical skills curriculum: a fixed core performed for every patient, plus one cluster of additional maneuvers selected by the presenting problem. We then compared one class taught under the framework against three earlier classes taught head-to-toe.

24core maneuvers, in 7 sections
8clusters, keyed to presenting problems
8.9points — margin of ordinary variation
+16points on the indicated maneuvers

What we found

Across four maneuvers scored with word-for-word identical criteria in both years, complete performance rose from 72.3% to 88.2%. Every one fell outside the margin of ordinary variation between classes.

What it cannot show

One case, four maneuvers, one school. Core + Cluster was added in the second semester rather than taught first, and we compared separate classes rather than randomizing. We scored completeness, not accuracy or reasoning.

Abstract

As presented, with the final core count of 24 examination maneuvers

Background

Physical examination teaching in early medical curricula commonly emphasizes a comprehensive head-to-toe approach. While foundational, this approach is time-intensive, inconsistently reflected in routine clinical practice, and misaligned with how clinicians select maneuvers in response to specific patient problems.

Summary of Work

We implemented a Core + Cluster physical examination framework, adapted from the model described by Gowda and colleagues. Faculty defined a Core examination comprising 24 maneuvers performed for all patients, with three universal items counted separately, and specified eight domain-based clusters, each adding maneuvers selected to support hypothesis-driven clinical reasoning. All maneuvers include full and partial performance descriptors with explicit, observable criteria.

Summary of Results

Across clusters, learners add between 3 and 20 targeted maneuvers beyond the core, averaging 9. Clusters are applied singly rather than cumulatively: each encounter comprises the core plus the one cluster indicated. This preserves a consistent examination baseline while modeling how the examination expands in response to evolving clinical hypotheses.

Discussion and Conclusion

By separating universal maneuvers from context-specific additions, the framework aligns physical examination teaching with authentic clinical practice while preserving foundational skills development. Explicit performance descriptors establish a shared language for teaching, feedback, and assessment.
